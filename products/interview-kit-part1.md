## Section 1: The Border Process Step by Step

### Understanding the Two Paths

There are two ways Canadian citizens apply for TN status at the border:

**Path A: Land Border Crossing (Port of Entry)**
- You drive to a U.S.-Canada land border crossing (e.g., Peace Bridge in Buffalo, Pacific Highway in Blaine, Ambassador Bridge in Detroit)
- You tell the primary inspection officer you are applying for TN status
- You pay the **$50 USD filing fee** (credit card or cash)
- You are directed to secondary inspection for your interview

**Path B: Airport (Pre-Clearance)**
- You arrive at a Canadian airport with U.S. pre-clearance (Toronto Pearson, Vancouver, Calgary, Montreal, Ottawa, Edmonton, Winnipeg, Halifax)
- You tell the CBP officer at pre-clearance that you are applying for TN status
- **2026 Change:** Canadian TN filings are now limited to CBP pre-clearance or preflight inspection stations **located within Canada only**. Pre-clearance facilities in Ireland, the Caribbean, or elsewhere no longer accept TN applications.
- No separate $50 filing fee at airports (processed through I-94 automation)

**Which should you choose?**

| Factor | Land Border | Airport Pre-Clearance |
|---|---|---|
| Cost | $50 filing fee | No separate fee |
| Wait times | Can be shorter on weekdays | Depends on flight schedule |
| Flexibility | Can try again same day if denied | Must rebook flight if denied |
| Pressure | Lower — you can drive home | Higher — you have a flight to catch |
| Best for | First-time applicants, complex cases | Renewals, straightforward cases |

**Pro tip:** For your first TN application, many immigration lawyers recommend a **land border crossing on a Tuesday, Wednesday, or Thursday morning**. Mondays and Fridays are busier. Weekday mornings tend to have more experienced officers and shorter wait times.

---

### Step 1: Arriving at the Border

**At a Land Crossing:**
1. Drive up to the primary inspection booth
2. Hand the officer your Canadian passport
3. When asked the purpose of your visit, say: **"I'm applying for TN status"** or **"I have a TN visa application"**
4. The officer will direct you to park and enter the secondary inspection office
5. Bring ALL your documents in a clearly organized folder or binder

**At an Airport (Pre-Clearance):**
1. Arrive at the airport **at least 3-4 hours before your flight** (this is critical — TN processing can take 1-3 hours)
2. Proceed to U.S. Customs and Border Protection pre-clearance
3. Tell the first officer: **"I'm applying for TN status"**
4. You will be directed to a secondary inspection area
5. Have all documents ready and organized

**What to bring (your document checklist):**
- ✅ Valid Canadian passport (not expiring within 6 months)
- ✅ Original employer support letter on company letterhead, **signed in wet ink** (not digital/e-signature)
- ✅ Copy of your degree(s) and/or diploma(s) — originals preferred
- ✅ Official transcripts (especially if your degree name doesn't obviously match your TN category)
- ✅ Professional licenses or certifications (if applicable to your profession)
- ✅ Foreign credential evaluation (if your degree is from outside the U.S., Canada, or Mexico)
- ✅ Resume/CV
- ✅ Previous I-94 records (if this is a renewal)
- ✅ Proof of ties to Canada (property deed, lease, family documentation — optional but helpful)
- ✅ $50 USD filing fee for land crossings (credit card or exact cash)

**2026 Critical Change — Employer Letter Requirements:**
Your employer support letter must now include ALL of the following:
- The specific TN profession title (must match the USMCA list exactly)
- A detailed description of your job duties (vague descriptions are now grounds for denial)
- The purpose of your entry to the U.S.
- The anticipated length of stay (with start and end dates)
- Your salary/compensation details
- The employer's U.S. address and contact information
- Confirmation that the employer is a **U.S.-based entity** (foreign companies with U.S. operations no longer qualify as sponsors)
- Printed on official company letterhead
- **Signed in original ink** (wet signature required — digital signatures are rejected)

---

### Step 2: Primary Inspection (1-5 Minutes)

Primary inspection is brief. This is the first officer you encounter — either at the booth (land border) or the first desk (airport pre-clearance).

**What happens:**
- The officer scans your passport
- They check your travel history and immigration record in their system
- They ask 1-3 basic questions (purpose of visit, citizenship)
- They direct you to secondary inspection

**What to say:**
- Keep it simple: "I'm a Canadian citizen applying for TN status to work in the United States."
- Do NOT launch into a detailed explanation of your job
- Do NOT hand over your entire document package yet
- Simply answer their questions and wait to be directed

**What the officer is checking:**
- Whether you have any flags in the system (prior denials, overstays, criminal records)
- Whether your passport is valid
- Basic admissibility screening

**Time:** 1-5 minutes typically. You will then be directed to secondary inspection.

---

### Step 3: Secondary Inspection — The Real Interview (15 Minutes to 2+ Hours)

This is where your TN application is actually adjudicated. A CBP officer will review your documents and question you.

**What happens step by step:**

1. **You wait.** Sometimes 10 minutes, sometimes 90 minutes. Bring something to read. Stay calm and patient. Do not use your phone excessively — officers notice.

2. **Your name is called.** Approach the officer's desk or window. Be polite, make eye contact, and greet them.

3. **You hand over your documents.** Present your employer letter, passport, degrees, and supporting documents in an organized manner. Have them in a clear folder with tabs if possible.

4. **The officer reviews your paperwork.** They may spend 5-30 minutes reading everything before asking you a single question. This is normal. Do not interrupt.

5. **The officer asks you questions.** This is the core of the interview. Questions will cover your job, qualifications, employer, and intent. (See Section 2 for the complete question list with ideal answers.)

6. **The officer may make phone calls.** They sometimes call your employer to verify details. Make sure a knowledgeable HR contact or your manager is available by phone during your application window. **Alert your employer in advance.**

7. **The officer may check your employer's website.** They frequently look up the company online. Make sure your company's website is professional and that your role/team is visible if possible.

8. **Decision time.** The officer will either:
   - **Approve** your application → You get photographed, fingerprinted, and receive an I-94 (proof of status)
   - **Request more information** → They may ask for additional documents
   - **Deny** your application → They will explain the reason; you can withdraw and reapply
   - **Offer withdrawal** → If things aren't going well, they may suggest you withdraw your application (this is better than a formal denial on your record)

**Upon approval:**
- The officer takes your photograph and fingerprints
- You receive an I-94 card (physical or electronic) — this is your proof of legal TN status
- Your TN status is typically granted for up to 3 years
- You can retrieve your electronic I-94 anytime at [i94.cbp.dhs.gov](https://i94.cbp.dhs.gov)

### Step 4: Timeline Expectations

| Scenario | Typical Time |
|---|---|
| Straightforward approval (clean case, experienced officer) | 15-45 minutes |
| Standard case with moderate questioning | 45-90 minutes |
| Complex case or busy port | 1.5-3 hours |
| Case requiring employer verification calls | 2-4 hours |
| Case sent to additional review | 2-6 hours (rare) |

**Plan for the worst case.** If you're at an airport, give yourself a minimum of 4 hours before your flight. If you're at a land border, clear your entire morning or afternoon.

### Step 5: 2026 Enhanced Vetting — What's New

The immigration landscape has changed significantly in 2026. Here's what you need to know:

**Stricter Documentation Standards**
- USCIS policy guidance updated in June 2025 is now fully in force
- Officers now scrutinize whether your **actual job duties** match the TN occupation list — a matching job title alone is no longer sufficient
- **Experience can no longer substitute for a required degree.** If your TN category requires a bachelor's degree, you must have one. Years of work experience will not be accepted as a replacement.
- Only **U.S.-based entities** can sponsor TN applicants. A foreign company with U.S. operations does not qualify.
- Self-employment remains strictly prohibited under TN status

**Enhanced Biometric Screening (Effective January 1, 2026)**
- Expanded biometric collection at all ports of entry
- Mandatory facial scans for all travelers
- Potential device searches (phones, laptops) — officers have broad authority
- Nationals of certain countries face additional screening layers

**Longer Processing Times**
- The 76-day DHS shutdown (ended late April 2026) created backlogs
- Enhanced vetting means officers are spending more time per case
- Social media checks are now routine — officers may review your LinkedIn, Facebook, and other public profiles
- Expect wait times 20-40% longer than pre-2026 levels at most ports

**Narrower Occupation Categories**
- The **Engineer** classification now requires credentials in a recognized engineering discipline (computer science alone may not qualify)
- **Scientific Technician/Technologist** is much narrower — patient care roles no longer qualify
- **Management Consultant** remains heavily scrutinized — officers challenge whether you're truly consulting vs. performing permanent employee duties
- All licensed professions (nurses, lawyers, physical therapists, veterinarians) must meet **state-specific licensing requirements** for the state where they'll work

**What this means for you:**
- Prepare more thoroughly than ever before
- Have your documents perfectly organized
- Practice your answers until they're natural and confident
- Arrive early and expect longer waits
- Make sure your social media profiles are professional and consistent with your application

### If You're Denied: What Happens Next

A denial is serious but not the end of the road. Key facts:

- A **denial** goes on your immigration record and can complicate future applications
- A **withdrawal** (if offered) does NOT go on your record — always accept a withdrawal over a denial if given the choice
- You **can reapply** after a denial, but you must address the specific reason for refusal
- There is **no formal appeal** for TN denials
- The FY 2024 Department of State denial rate was **42.63%** (primarily Mexican applicants through consular processing)
- The USCIS denial rate for employer-sponsored cases is much lower at **5.2%** (FY 2025)
- For Canadians at the border, approval rates for well-prepared cases exceed **99%**

**Top reasons for denial:**
1. Job duties don't match the TN occupation category
2. Missing or incomplete documentation (especially the employer letter)
3. Qualifications don't meet the minimum requirements for the profession
4. Failure to demonstrate nonimmigrant intent (ties to Canada)
5. Conflicting answers during the interview
6. Criminal history or past immigration violations
7. Evidence of pursuing permanent residence (green card)
8. Position not at a professional level (salary too low, duties too basic)
9. Employer letter uses vague or inconsistent language

**If denied, before reapplying:**
1. Get the specific reason in writing
2. Address that exact issue in your new application
3. Strongly consider hiring an immigration lawyer
4. Do NOT simply resubmit the same application — this will result in another denial
5. Be cautious about changing key details for the same employer/position — officers cross-reference prior applications

## Section 2: 35+ Common CBP Officer Questions with Ideal Answers

### How to Use This Section

For every question below, you'll find:
- **The Question** — exactly as the officer may phrase it
- **Why They Ask** — the underlying concern driving the question
- **Ideal Answer** — a concise, confident response template (customize with your details)
- **🚫 What NOT to Say** — common mistakes that raise red flags

**Three golden rules for answering ALL questions:**
1. **Be brief.** Answer with "yes" or "no" when appropriate. If the officer wants more detail, they will ask. Do not volunteer extra information.
2. **Be direct.** No dancing around questions. No hedging. Officers expect straight answers.
3. **Be truthful.** Never lie or exaggerate. Lying to a CBP officer can result in permanent bars from entry to the United States. If you don't know something, say "I'm not sure, but I can find out."

---

### Category 1: About You (7 Questions)

These questions establish your identity, background, and admissibility. Officers ask them to verify your passport information, check for red flags, and set the tone for the interview.

---

**Question 1: "What is your citizenship?"**

**Why they ask:** This is the foundational eligibility question. TN status is only available to citizens of Canada and Mexico. They need to confirm you qualify before reviewing anything else.

**Ideal answer:** "I'm a Canadian citizen." *(Hand over your passport if you haven't already.)*

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "I'm a permanent resident of Canada" — permanent residents do NOT qualify for TN status; you must be a citizen
- Don't mention dual citizenship with a third country unless directly asked — it can trigger additional screening
- Don't say "I was born in [another country] but I'm Canadian now" — just state your citizenship simply

---

**Question 2: "What is your full name?"**

**Why they ask:** They're verifying your identity matches your passport and application documents. They're also checking their database for any records under your name.

**Ideal answer:** State your full legal name exactly as it appears on your passport. "My name is [First Name] [Middle Name] [Last Name]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't use nicknames or shortened versions of your name
- Don't give a different name order than what's on your passport
- If your name has changed (marriage, legal change), have supporting documentation ready and mention it proactively: "My legal name is [X], formerly [Y]. I have my name change documentation here."

---

**Question 3: "Where do you live in Canada?"**

**Why they ask:** They want to establish your ties to Canada and your current residence. This feeds into the nonimmigrant intent assessment — do you have a home to return to?

**Ideal answer:** "I live in [City, Province]. I [own a home / rent an apartment] there." Keep it simple and specific.

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "I've already moved most of my things to the U.S." — this signals immigrant intent
- Don't say "I don't really have a permanent address right now" — this undermines your ties to Canada
- Don't be vague — "somewhere in Ontario" sounds evasive

---

**Question 4: "Have you been to the United States before?"**

**Why they ask:** They're checking your travel history for consistency with their records. They can see your entry/exit history on their screen. They also want to know if you've previously worked in the U.S. or had any immigration issues.

**Ideal answer:** Be honest and brief. "Yes, I've visited for [tourism/business/previous TN status] several times." Or: "Yes, I previously held TN status with [employer] from [date] to [date]." Or if it's your first time: "No, this would be my first visit."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't lie about previous visits — they can see your entire travel history
- Don't say "I've been living in the U.S. on and off" if you were on tourist status — this suggests you may have been working illegally
- Don't omit previous TN applications or other visa applications — they have records

---

**Question 5: "Have you ever been denied entry to the United States?"**

**Why they ask:** Prior denials are a major red flag. They want to know if you've had immigration problems before and whether the issues have been resolved. They already have this information in their system — they're testing your honesty.

**Ideal answer:** If no: "No, I have never been denied entry."
If yes: "Yes, I was denied entry on [date] at [port] because [brief, honest reason]. I've since [resolved the issue / obtained proper documentation]." Have supporting documents ready.

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- NEVER lie about a prior denial — they can see it on their screen and lying will result in immediate denial and potential permanent consequences
- Don't be defensive or blame the previous officer
- Don't say "I was denied but it was unfair" — just state the facts

---

**Question 6: "Do you have a criminal record?"**

**Why they ask:** Certain criminal convictions can make you inadmissible to the United States. Even minor offenses (including DUIs, which are treated more seriously in U.S. immigration law than in Canada) can be an issue. They have access to Canadian criminal databases through information-sharing agreements.

**Ideal answer:** If no: "No, I do not have a criminal record."
If yes: Be honest and prepared. "I had a [specific charge] in [year]. It was [resolved/discharged/pardoned]. I have documentation of the disposition here." Bring court records, proof of completion of any sentences, and pardons if applicable.

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't lie — U.S. and Canadian law enforcement share criminal record information
- Don't minimize by saying "nothing serious" — let the officer assess the severity
- Don't volunteer information about charges that were withdrawn or where you were found not guilty, unless directly asked about arrests (as opposed to convictions)
- Don't say "just a DUI" — DUIs are taken very seriously in U.S. immigration

---

**Question 7: "Are you currently employed in Canada?"**

**Why they ask:** They want to understand your current situation. If you're currently employed, it shows stability. If you're unemployed, they may wonder about your motivation and whether you're truly coming for a temporary professional opportunity or seeking to relocate permanently.

**Ideal answer:** If employed: "Yes, I currently work as a [title] at [company] in [city]. I'll be leaving that position to take this role in the U.S."
If not currently employed: "I recently completed my role at [company]. I'm transitioning to this new position in the United States." Frame it positively.

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "I've been unemployed for a long time and really need this job" — this sounds desperate and suggests immigrant intent
- Don't say "I quit my job already to move to the U.S." — this signals you've already committed to relocating permanently
- Don't lie about current employment — they may verify

### Category 2: About Your Job (10 Questions)

This is the most important category. Officers spend the most time here because they need to verify that your job genuinely fits a TN occupation category, that the employer is legitimate, and that the role is professional-level. In 2026, with stricter USCIS guidance in force, officers are paying closer attention to whether your **actual duties** match the TN profession — not just the job title.

---

**Question 8: "Why are you coming to the United States?"**

**Why they ask:** This is your opening statement. They want a clear, concise explanation of your purpose. This sets the frame for the entire interview.

**Ideal answer:** "I'm applying for TN status to work as a [TN profession title] for [company name] in [city, state]. They've offered me a [duration]-year position as a [job title]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "to live in the U.S." or "to start a new life" — this signals immigrant intent
- Don't say "to make more money" — while true, it sounds like economic migration rather than a professional opportunity
- Don't give a long, rambling answer — keep it to 2-3 sentences maximum
- Don't say "my company is transferring me" unless it's actually an intra-company transfer (that would be an L-1 visa, not TN)

---

**Question 9: "What will you be doing?"**

**Why they ask:** This is the critical question. They need to verify that your daily activities match the TN occupation category you're applying under. In 2026, officers are trained to probe beyond job titles and look at actual duties.

**Ideal answer:** Describe your duties in 3-4 sentences using language that directly mirrors the USMCA occupation description. For example: "As a Computer Systems Analyst, I'll be analyzing the company's existing computer systems, evaluating system requirements, and recommending technology solutions. I'll be documenting system specifications and working with the development team to implement improvements."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't describe duties that don't match your TN category (e.g., don't say "I'll be writing code" if you're applying as a Computer Systems Analyst — programming is not systems analysis)
- Don't be vague: "I'll be doing tech stuff" will get you more questions or a denial
- Don't describe duties that sound like a non-professional role: "I'll be helping out with whatever they need"
- Don't contradict what's in your employer letter — officers will cross-reference your verbal answers with the written description

---

**Question 10: "What is your job title?"**

**Why they ask:** They're checking that your job title aligns with a recognized TN profession on the USMCA list. They'll compare this to your employer letter.

**Ideal answer:** State the title exactly as it appears in your employer letter, and connect it to the TN category: "My title is [Job Title]. I'm applying under the [TN Profession] category."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't give a different title than what's in your employer letter
- Don't use informal titles: "I'm basically a tech lead" — use the formal title
- Don't use a title that doesn't exist on the TN professions list without explaining how it maps to an approved category

---

**Question 11: "Describe your daily duties." / "Walk me through a typical workday."**

**Why they ask:** This is the deep-dive question. They want to hear, in your own words, what you actually do. They're testing whether you truly understand the role and whether the duties are consistent with the TN profession. This is where many applicants stumble.

**Ideal answer:** Walk through a realistic day using professional language that maps to your TN category. For example: "A typical day starts with reviewing system performance reports and meeting with stakeholders to discuss their technology needs. I spend the afternoon analyzing data workflows, documenting system requirements, and preparing recommendations for system improvements. I also coordinate with the development team on implementation timelines."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't describe duties outside your TN category (if you're a Management Consultant, don't describe managing employees day-to-day — that's a manager, not a consultant)
- Don't say "I'm not sure yet, I haven't started" — you should know what you'll be doing
- Don't describe an entry-level or non-professional role: "I'll be answering phones and doing data entry"
- Don't memorize a script that sounds robotic — it should sound natural and knowledgeable

---

**Question 12: "Who is your employer?"**

**Why they ask:** They need to verify the employer is a legitimate U.S.-based entity. In 2026, only U.S.-based entities can sponsor TN applicants. They may look up the company on their computer during the interview.

**Ideal answer:** "[Company Name]. They're a [brief description — e.g., 'technology company' or 'healthcare organization'] based in [city, state]. They have approximately [X] employees."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't be unable to describe what the company does — officers will question whether the job offer is real
- Don't say it's a foreign company — even if it has U.S. operations, the sponsoring entity must be U.S.-based
- Don't say "it's a startup with just the founder" unless you can clearly demonstrate it's a legitimate operation with professional-level work

---

**Question 13: "What does the company do?"**

**Why they ask:** They want to verify the company is real, legitimate, and that it makes sense for them to hire someone in your TN profession. They will often check the company website during the interview.

**Ideal answer:** Give a clear, 2-3 sentence description: "[Company] is a [industry] company that [core business activity]. They serve [type of clients/customers] and are headquartered in [location]." Make sure this matches what's on the company's website.

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't be vague: "They do consulting" or "They're in tech" — be specific
- Don't describe the company in a way that contradicts their website
- Don't say "I'm not really sure what they do" — this is a major red flag that suggests the job offer may not be genuine

---

**Question 14: "How many employees does the company have?"**

**Why they ask:** Company size helps them assess legitimacy. A very small company hiring a TN professional may face more scrutiny (is there really enough professional-level work?). They're also checking if your answer matches what they can find online.

**Ideal answer:** "The company has approximately [number] employees." Give your best accurate estimate. If it's a large company, you can say "over [X] employees globally, with about [Y] in the U.S. office where I'll be working."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't wildly guess — if you say 500 and the company website says 50, that's a credibility problem
- Don't say "I don't know" — research this before your interview
- If it's a very small company (under 10), be prepared for follow-up questions about whether there's enough professional work to justify the role

---

**Question 15: "What is your salary?"**

**Why they ask:** Salary is an indicator of whether the position is truly professional-level. A TN profession that pays significantly below market rate raises questions about whether the role is genuinely professional. They also want to verify consistency with your employer letter.

**Ideal answer:** "My annual salary will be [amount] USD." State the figure confidently. It should match your employer letter exactly.

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't give a different number than what's in your employer letter
- Don't say "we haven't finalized compensation yet" — your offer should be concrete
- Don't quote an unusually low salary for your profession — if an Engineer is being paid $35,000/year, the officer will question whether this is truly a professional role
- Don't mention equity, bonuses, or complex compensation structures unless asked — keep it simple with the base salary

---

**Question 16: "Where will you be working?"**

**Why they ask:** They need to know the physical work location. For licensed professions, the state matters because licensing requirements vary by state. They also want to verify the employer has a real office.

**Ideal answer:** "I'll be working at [company name]'s office in [city, state], at [street address if you know it]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "I'll be working remotely from Canada" — you need to be working IN the United States on TN status
- Don't say "various locations" without being specific — if you'll travel, name the primary office
- Don't be unable to name the city and state — this suggests you haven't thought through the logistics
- If you'll work remotely from a U.S. location, clarify: "I'll be working from [U.S. city] with occasional visits to the [city] office"

---

**Question 17: "Who will you report to?"**

**Why they ask:** They want to verify the organizational structure and that you'll be supervised by someone at the company. This helps confirm the employer-employee relationship and that the role is legitimate.

**Ideal answer:** "I'll report to [Name], who is the [Title — e.g., VP of Engineering, Director of Operations] at [company]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "I don't know yet" — find out before your interview
- Don't say "no one, I'll be independent" — this sounds like self-employment, which is prohibited under TN
- Don't describe a reporting structure that suggests you're a contractor rather than an employee (unless your TN category supports consulting arrangements)


---

### Category 3: About Your Qualifications (7 Questions)

These questions verify that you meet the minimum educational and professional requirements for your TN category. In 2026, this is more critical than ever: **experience can no longer substitute for a required degree**, and officers are checking credentials more carefully.

---

**Question 18: "What is your education?"**

**Why they ask:** Every TN profession has specific educational requirements. The officer needs to confirm you hold the required degree. Under the 2026 rules, you must have the **exact credential** required — no substitutions.

**Ideal answer:** "I have a [Bachelor's/Master's] degree in [field] from [university name], which I completed in [year]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "I have the equivalent of a degree through work experience" — experience substitution is no longer accepted as of the June 2025 USCIS policy update
- Don't be vague about your degree field — "I studied business" is less convincing than "I have a Bachelor of Commerce with a major in Accounting"
- Don't mention incomplete degrees or degrees in progress

---

**Question 19: "Where did you go to school?"**

**Why they ask:** They want to verify the institution is legitimate and that your degree is real. For foreign institutions (outside U.S./Canada/Mexico), they'll want to see a credential evaluation.

**Ideal answer:** "[University name] in [city, country]. It's a [brief descriptor if helpful — e.g., 'public research university' or 'accredited institution']." If your school is well-known (University of Toronto, McGill, UBC), the name alone is sufficient.

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't name an institution you didn't actually attend
- Don't be unable to provide basic details about your school if asked follow-up questions
- If your degree is from outside North America, proactively mention: "I also have a credential evaluation from [evaluation service] confirming it's equivalent to a U.S. [degree type]"

---

**Question 20: "What was your degree in?"**

**Why they ask:** The degree field must align with the TN profession. An Engineer needs an engineering degree. An Accountant needs an accounting degree. Mismatches between degree field and TN category are one of the top reasons for denial.

**Ideal answer:** "My degree is in [specific field]." If your degree name doesn't perfectly match your TN category, be prepared to explain the connection: "My degree is in Information Systems, which covered [relevant coursework] directly applicable to my role as a Computer Systems Analyst."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't name a degree field that has no obvious connection to your TN category without explanation
- Don't say "it was a general degree" — specificity matters
- Don't claim a degree you don't have — officers may ask to see the original diploma or transcripts

---

**Question 21: "Do you have any professional licenses or certifications?"**

**Why they ask:** Some TN professions require specific licenses (nurses, lawyers, accountants, engineers, physical therapists, veterinarians). Even for professions that don't require licenses, relevant certifications strengthen your case. In 2026, licensed professions must meet **state-specific requirements** for the state where they'll work.

**Ideal answer:** If yes: "Yes, I hold a [license/certification name] issued by [issuing body]. It's valid in [jurisdiction]." Bring the original or a certified copy.
If no (and not required): "My TN category doesn't require a specific license, but I do hold [relevant certification] which demonstrates my expertise in this area."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't claim licenses you don't have
- Don't say "I'm planning to get licensed" if the profession requires it — you need the license before applying
- Don't forget to mention relevant certifications — they strengthen your case
- If your profession requires state licensing, don't show up without it — this is an automatic denial

---

**Question 22: "How many years of experience do you have?"**

**Why they ask:** Experience demonstrates that you're genuinely qualified for a professional-level role. While experience alone can no longer substitute for a degree, it still matters for establishing credibility.

**Ideal answer:** "[X] years. I've worked as a [relevant roles] at [companies], focusing on [relevant areas]." Keep it concise but specific.

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't exaggerate your experience — if they ask follow-up questions, inconsistencies will surface
- Don't say "I just graduated and this is my first job" without confidence — while new graduates can qualify, frame it positively: "I completed my degree in [field] in [year] and this role aligns directly with my education"
- Don't describe experience that contradicts your TN category

---

**Question 23: "What makes you qualified for this role?"**

**Why they ask:** This is your chance to connect the dots: your education + your experience + the job requirements. They want to hear a coherent story about why you're the right professional for this position.

**Ideal answer:** "I have a [degree] in [field] from [university], [X] years of experience in [relevant area], and [specific skills/certifications]. My background in [specific expertise] directly aligns with the [TN profession] duties I'll be performing at [company]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't be unable to articulate why you're qualified — this is a fundamental question
- Don't focus on soft skills: "I'm a hard worker and a team player" — focus on credentials and technical qualifications
- Don't say "the company thinks I'm qualified" — YOU need to demonstrate it
- Don't mention qualifications that are irrelevant to the TN category

---

**Question 24: "How does your degree relate to your job?"**

**Why they ask:** This is especially common when the degree name doesn't perfectly match the TN profession. Officers want to see a clear, logical connection between what you studied and what you'll be doing.

**Ideal answer:** "My degree in [field] covered [specific relevant coursework — name 2-3 courses or areas]. These directly apply to my role as a [TN profession] because [brief explanation of how the knowledge is used in the job]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "it's not directly related but I learned on the job" — in 2026, the degree must match
- Don't be unable to name specific coursework that connects to your role
- Don't say "my degree is in a completely different field" — if this is true, you may not qualify and should consult a lawyer before applying
- Bring your transcripts as backup — if the officer questions the connection, you can show specific courses


---

### Category 4: About Your Intent (7 Questions)

These are the questions that trip up the most applicants. The TN visa is a **nonimmigrant** visa — you must demonstrate that your stay is temporary and that you intend to return to Canada. Officers are trained to probe for signs of immigrant intent. Your answers here can make or break your application.

**The key principle:** You are coming to the U.S. for a **temporary professional assignment** with a **defined end date**. You maintain ties to Canada. You have no plans to permanently immigrate.

---

**Question 25: "How long will you be in the United States?"**

**Why they ask:** TN status is temporary (up to 3 years per approval). They want to confirm your stay has a defined duration that matches your employer letter.

**Ideal answer:** "My employment contract is for [duration — e.g., 'three years'], from [start date] to [end date], as stated in my employer letter."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "indefinitely" or "as long as possible" — this signals immigrant intent
- Don't say "forever" or "I'm not sure when I'll come back" — immediate red flag
- Don't say a duration longer than 3 years (the maximum TN period)
- Don't say "I'll keep renewing" — while TN can be renewed indefinitely, saying this suggests permanent intent. Focus on the current period only.

---

**Question 26: "When does your employment end?"**

**Why they ask:** They want to verify that the position is temporary with a defined end date. This is a core requirement of TN status.

**Ideal answer:** "My position runs through [end date], as specified in my employer letter." If it's a project-based role: "The project is expected to be completed by [date]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "it's a permanent position" — even if the company considers it permanent internally, your TN application must frame it as temporary
- Don't say "there's no end date" — your employer letter must include one
- Don't contradict the dates in your employer letter

---

**Question 27: "Will you return to Canada?"**

**Why they ask:** This is the direct nonimmigrant intent question. They want to hear that you plan to leave the U.S. when your TN status ends.

**Ideal answer:** "Yes, absolutely. I plan to return to Canada when my assignment is complete. I maintain [my home/apartment, family ties, bank accounts, professional connections] in Canada."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't hesitate or seem uncertain — confidence matters here
- Don't say "probably" or "I hope so" — say "yes"
- Don't say "it depends on whether I get a green card" — this is an immediate denial trigger
- Don't say "I don't have anything to go back to" — this destroys your nonimmigrant intent argument

---

**Question 28: "Do you own property in Canada?"**

**Why they ask:** Property ownership is one of the strongest indicators of ties to your home country. It suggests you have roots and a reason to return.

**Ideal answer:** If yes: "Yes, I own [a home/condo] in [city]. I [will be keeping it / have it rented out while I'm away]."
If no: "I don't own property, but I maintain my apartment lease in [city] and have [other ties — family, bank accounts, professional memberships, etc.]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "I sold my house to move to the U.S." — this signals permanent relocation
- Don't say "I don't own anything in Canada" without offering alternative ties
- Don't lie about owning property — if they ask for documentation, you'll be caught

---

**Question 29: "Do you have family in Canada?"**

**Why they ask:** Family ties are another strong indicator of nonimmigrant intent. Having a spouse, children, parents, or other close family in Canada suggests you have reasons to return.

**Ideal answer:** If yes: "Yes, my [parents/siblings/spouse/children] live in [city, Canada]. I'll be visiting them regularly."
If your family is coming with you: "My [spouse/children] will be accompanying me on TD status, but we maintain our home and family connections in Canada."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "all my family is already in the U.S." — this weakens your ties to Canada
- Don't say "I don't have any family" without offering other ties to Canada
- Don't mention family members who are undocumented or have had immigration issues in the U.S.

---

**Question 30: "Have you applied for a green card?"**

**Why they ask:** This is a direct test of immigrant intent. Having a pending green card application while applying for TN status is a major red flag, though it's not automatically disqualifying. The legal standard is nuanced — TN requires nonimmigrant intent, but "dual intent" is a gray area.

**Ideal answer:** If no: "No, I have not applied for a green card and have no plans to do so."
If yes (this is complex territory): "My employer has begun the PERM process, but my current intent is to work temporarily under TN status and return to Canada when my assignment ends." **Note: If you have a pending green card application, strongly consider consulting an immigration lawyer before your TN interview.**

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't lie about a pending green card application — they can check USCIS records
- Don't say "yes, I'm hoping to get my green card soon" — this directly contradicts nonimmigrant intent
- Don't volunteer information about green card plans if not asked
- Don't say "my employer wants to sponsor me for a green card" unless directly asked

---

**Question 31: "Do you plan to apply for permanent residence?"**

**Why they ask:** Similar to the green card question, but forward-looking. They want to know your future intentions. This is where many applicants accidentally sabotage themselves.

**Ideal answer:** "No, I don't have plans to apply for permanent residence. I'm focused on this temporary professional opportunity and plan to return to Canada afterward."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "eventually, yes" or "maybe someday" — keep the focus on your temporary intent
- Don't say "that's the long-term plan" — this is an admission of immigrant intent
- Don't discuss your immigration strategy or future visa plans
- Don't say "my lawyer is working on it" — this is essentially admitting immigrant intent


---

### Category 5: About Your Dependents (4 Questions)

If you're bringing family members, officers will ask about them. Your spouse and unmarried children under 21 can accompany you on **TD (Trade Dependent) status**. These questions assess whether your family situation is consistent with temporary intent.

---

**Question 32: "Is your spouse coming with you?"**

**Why they ask:** They need to know if dependents will be applying for TD status. They also assess whether your entire family relocating suggests permanent intent.

**Ideal answer:** If yes: "Yes, my spouse will be accompanying me on TD status. [He/She] will be joining me in [city]."
If no: "No, my spouse will remain in Canada in [city]. [He/She] works at [employer] there."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "my spouse is already living in the U.S." if they don't have legal status — this creates complications
- Don't say "my spouse will find a job in the U.S." — TD status holders **cannot work** in the United States
- Don't be unclear about your spouse's plans — have a clear, consistent answer

---

**Question 33: "Will your children attend school in the United States?"**

**Why they ask:** Children on TD status can attend school in the U.S. (elementary through university). However, enrolling children in school can be seen as a sign of settling in permanently, so frame it as temporary.

**Ideal answer:** "Yes, my children will attend school in [city] during my assignment. They're currently enrolled in [school in Canada] and will return there when we go back."
Or: "My children are young and will attend [elementary/middle school] near our temporary residence in [city]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "we've already enrolled them and bought a house near the school" — this sounds permanent
- Don't say "they'll be applying to U.S. colleges" — this suggests long-term plans
- Frame everything as temporary arrangements during your assignment

---

**Question 34: "Does your spouse plan to work in the United States?"**

**Why they ask:** This is a critical question. **TD status holders are NOT authorized to work in the United States.** If your spouse wants to work, they need their own work visa (such as their own TN, H-1B, etc.).

**Ideal answer:** "No, my spouse will not be working in the United States. [He/She] plans to [take care of our children / pursue personal interests / volunteer / take courses] during our time there."
Or if your spouse has their own work authorization: "My spouse has [his/her] own [visa type] and is authorized to work independently."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- NEVER say "yes, my spouse will work" if they'll be on TD status — this is illegal and will raise serious concerns
- Don't say "my spouse will work remotely for a Canadian company" — this is a gray area that can cause problems; consult a lawyer
- Don't say "we'll figure it out when we get there"

---

**Question 35: "Will any other family members be accompanying you?"**

**Why they ask:** They want a complete picture of who is entering the U.S. with you and on what status. Only your spouse and unmarried children under 21 qualify for TD status.

**Ideal answer:** "Yes, my [spouse and X children] will accompany me on TD status." Or: "No, I'll be traveling alone. My family will remain in Canada."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't mention extended family members (parents, siblings) as accompanying you — they don't qualify for TD status
- Don't say adult children over 21 will be on TD status — they don't qualify
- Don't be vague about who is coming — have a clear answer

---

### Category 6: Follow-Up and Miscellaneous Questions (4 Questions)

Officers may ask additional questions based on your specific situation or to clarify something from your earlier answers. These are less predictable but commonly reported.

---

**Question 36: "Is this your first TN visa?"**

**Why they ask:** If you've had multiple TNs, they want to understand the pattern. Multiple consecutive TNs over many years can raise questions about whether your stay is truly "temporary."

**Ideal answer:** If first time: "Yes, this is my first TN application."
If renewal: "No, I've held TN status [X] times previously. My most recent TN was with [employer] from [date] to [date]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't lie about previous TNs — they can see your complete history
- If you've had many consecutive TNs (5+ years), don't say "I've been on TN for 10 years" without being prepared to explain why your stay is still temporary
- Don't say "I've lost count" — know your history

---

**Question 37: "Have you held other U.S. visas before?"**

**Why they ask:** They want your complete immigration history. Previous visa types, overstays, or violations will show up in their system.

**Ideal answer:** If yes: "Yes, I previously held [visa type] status from [date] to [date] for [purpose]."
If no: "No, this is my first U.S. visa application."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't omit previous visas — they have records
- Don't fail to mention if you overstayed a previous visa — address it proactively with documentation showing it was resolved
- Don't mention visa applications that were denied without being prepared to explain the circumstances

---

**Question 38: "Where will you be living in the United States?"**

**Why they ask:** They want to confirm you have a plan and that your living situation is consistent with temporary status.

**Ideal answer:** "I'll be living in [city/neighborhood]. I've [secured a rental apartment / will be staying in temporary housing while I find an apartment]."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't say "I just bought a house" — while not disqualifying, it can raise immigrant intent concerns
- Don't say "I have no idea" — have at least a general plan
- Don't mention that you've moved all your belongings from Canada

---

**Question 39: "How did you find this job?"**

**Why they ask:** They want to understand the legitimacy of the job offer. A natural job search story (recruiter, job board, professional network) is reassuring. An unusual story may prompt more scrutiny.

**Ideal answer:** "I found the position through [LinkedIn / a recruiter / the company's website / a professional contact]. I went through [their standard interview process] and received an offer."

**🚫 What NOT to say:**
- Don't describe an arrangement that sounds like self-employment: "I convinced them to hire me as a consultant"
- Don't say "a friend set it up" without more context — it can sound like a fraudulent arrangement
- Don't describe a process that sounds unusual or informal

## Quick-Reference Cheat Sheet

Print this page and review it the morning of your interview.

### The 5 Things Every CBP Officer Wants to Confirm:

| # | What They're Checking | How You Prove It |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | You are a Canadian or Mexican citizen | Valid passport |
| 2 | You have a real job offer from a U.S. employer | Employer support letter (wet ink, letterhead) |
| 3 | The job fits a TN profession on the USMCA list | Job duties description matching the category |
| 4 | You have the required qualifications | Degree, transcripts, licenses, certifications |
| 5 | Your stay is temporary (nonimmigrant intent) | End date on offer, ties to Canada |

### Interview Do's:
- ✅ Dress in business casual — you're a professional applying for a professional visa
- ✅ Make eye contact and be polite
- ✅ Answer questions briefly and directly
- ✅ Tell the truth — always
- ✅ Have documents organized in a clear folder with tabs
- ✅ Alert your employer that CBP may call to verify details
- ✅ Arrive early (4 hours before flight at airports; clear your morning at land borders)
- ✅ Stay calm if the officer seems skeptical — it's their job to probe
- ✅ Know your employer letter contents inside and out

### Interview Don'ts:
- 🚫 Don't volunteer extra information — answer only what's asked
- 🚫 Don't argue with the officer — ever
- 🚫 Don't lie or exaggerate — about anything
- 🚫 Don't use your phone excessively while waiting
- 🚫 Don't interrupt the officer
- 🚫 Don't express intent to permanently live in the U.S.
- 🚫 Don't mention green card plans
- 🚫 Don't contradict your employer letter
- 🚫 Don't show up without original documents
- 🚫 Don't panic if sent to secondary inspection — it's standard for TN applications

### If Things Go Wrong:
1. **Stay calm.** Getting emotional or argumentative will make things worse.
2. **If offered a withdrawal, take it.** A withdrawal does NOT go on your record. A denial DOES.
3. **Ask for the specific reason.** You're entitled to know why you were denied.
4. **Don't argue the decision.** Thank the officer and leave.
5. **Consult an immigration lawyer** before reapplying.
6. **You can reapply** — but address the specific issue first.
