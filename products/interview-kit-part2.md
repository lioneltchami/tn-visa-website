# TN Visa Border Interview Kit
## Part 2: Profession-Specific Prep & Emergency Scenarios

*Last updated: April 2026*

> **Disclaimer:** This guide is for informational purposes only and does not constitute legal advice. Immigration laws and officer discretion vary. Consult a licensed immigration attorney for your specific situation.

## Profession-Specific Question Sets

Each profession below includes the most likely questions a CBP officer will ask at the border, along with ideal response strategies. Officers are trained to verify three things: (1) your qualifications match the USMCA profession, (2) your job duties align with that profession, and (3) you intend to stay temporarily.

---

### 1. Engineer

**Key context:** The June 2025 USCIS Policy Manual update tightened the Engineer category. A general Computer Science degree alone is no longer sufficient — you need a degree in a *bona fide engineering specialty* (e.g., Software Engineering, Electrical Engineering) or a professional engineering license. Officers are now explicitly checking for this.

**Q: What is your degree in?**
> **Ideal answer:** State your exact degree title. If it's in an engineering field (Civil, Mechanical, Electrical, Software Engineering, etc.), say so clearly. If your degree says "Computer Science," be prepared to explain how your program was engineering-focused or present your professional engineering license (P.Eng, PE).

**Q: What engineering duties will you perform?**
> **Ideal answer:** Describe duties that match the U.S. Department of Labor's Occupational Outlook Handbook (OOH) definition of engineering — designing systems, analyzing specifications, testing prototypes, solving technical problems. Avoid saying "coding" or "programming" without tying it to engineering design work.

**Q: Your degree says Computer Science. How is that engineering?**
> **Ideal answer:** "My program included [specific engineering coursework — systems design, hardware architecture, engineering mathematics]. I also hold a [P.Eng/PE license or ABET-accredited program completion]." If you don't have an engineering license or an engineering-titled degree, this is a high-risk category — consider Computer Systems Analyst instead.

**Q: Are you a licensed engineer?**
> **Ideal answer:** If yes, present your license. If no, explain that your baccalaureate degree is in an engineering specialty, which satisfies the USMCA requirement. Note: a state/provincial license can substitute for a degree in some cases.

**Q: Will you be writing code?**
> **Ideal answer:** "My role involves engineering design and analysis. While I use programming as a tool within that engineering work — similar to how a civil engineer uses CAD software — my primary duties are [designing systems/analyzing requirements/engineering solutions]."

**Q: What's the difference between what you do and what a programmer does?**
> **Ideal answer:** "A programmer implements specifications they're given. As an engineer, I design the systems, determine the technical requirements, create the architecture, and solve complex engineering problems. Writing code is one tool I use, but the core of my work is engineering design and analysis."

**Q: How long will you be working in the U.S.?**
> **Ideal answer:** State the specific duration on your offer letter. "My assignment is for [X months/years] as stated in my employment letter." Reinforce temporary intent.

### 2. Computer Systems Analyst

**Key context:** This is one of the most common TN categories for tech workers. The critical distinction is between *analysis* (qualifying) and *programming* (not qualifying under this category). Officers are trained to probe whether you're really a programmer being called an "analyst."

**Q: What does a Computer Systems Analyst do?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I analyze an organization's computer systems and business processes to recommend technology solutions that improve efficiency. I evaluate current systems, identify requirements, design system specifications, and coordinate implementation — but I'm not primarily writing code."

**Q: How is your role different from a programmer?**
> **Ideal answer:** "A programmer writes code based on specifications someone else creates. As a systems analyst, I'm the one creating those specifications. I study business needs, evaluate technology options, design system architectures, and write the requirements that developers follow. My work is analytical and consultative."

**Q: Will you be writing any code?**
> **Ideal answer:** "My primary duties are systems analysis, requirements gathering, and solution design. I may review code or create prototypes to validate my analysis, but coding is not my principal activity. My employer letter details my specific analytical duties."

**Q: What is your educational background?**
> **Ideal answer:** State your degree. A bachelor's degree in Computer Science, Information Systems, Business Administration with IT focus, or a related field qualifies. The key is connecting your education to *systems analysis*, not just programming.

**Q: Describe a typical day in this role.**
> **Ideal answer:** "I meet with business stakeholders to understand their needs, analyze current system performance, document requirements for new solutions, evaluate vendor products, create system design specifications, and present recommendations to management. I bridge the gap between business needs and technology solutions."

**Q: What systems will you be analyzing?**
> **Ideal answer:** Be specific about the employer's systems — ERP, CRM, cloud infrastructure, data platforms, etc. Show you understand the analytical scope of the role, not just the technology.

### 3. Management Consultant

**Key context:** This is one of the most heavily scrutinized TN categories. CBP officers know it's often used as a "catch-all." You must prove: (1) you're genuinely *consulting* (improving organizational performance), not just filling a permanent employee role, and (2) you have the qualifications. You can qualify with a bachelor's degree OR five years of experience as a management consultant OR five years in a related specialty field.

**Q: What consulting services will you provide?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I will analyze [specific business function — operations, supply chain, HR processes] and provide recommendations to improve [efficiency/revenue/cost reduction]. My engagement has a defined scope: [describe deliverables — process audit, implementation roadmap, training program]. This is a temporary consulting project, not an ongoing operational role."

**Q: Are you an employee or a contractor?**
> **Ideal answer:** If independent contractor: "I'm an independent consultant engaged under a consulting agreement with a defined project scope and timeline." If employee of a consulting firm: "I'm employed by [consulting firm], which has a contract with [U.S. client] for this specific engagement." If direct employee: "I'm engaged in a supernumerary capacity to complete a specific consulting project — I'm not filling an existing permanent position."

**Q: How is this different from just being a manager?**
> **Ideal answer:** "A manager performs ongoing operational duties. As a consultant, I'm brought in temporarily to analyze problems, design improvements, and implement changes. Once the project is complete and the organization can sustain the improvements independently, my engagement ends."

**Q: What qualifies you as a management consultant?**
> **Ideal answer:** Option A: "I hold a [bachelor's/MBA] degree in [field]." Option B: "I have [X] years of documented experience as a management consultant, working with [list clients/industries]." Option C: "I have [X] years of experience in [specialty field] directly related to this consulting engagement." Have supporting documentation for whichever path you claim.

**Q: When does this consulting engagement end?**
> **Ideal answer:** Give a specific date or timeframe. "The project scope is [X months], with completion targeted for [date]. My consulting agreement specifies this timeline." Vague or open-ended answers raise red flags.

**Q: Who are your other consulting clients?**
> **Ideal answer:** If you have them, list them — multiple clients strengthen the "genuine consultant" narrative. If this is your only client, emphasize the project-based nature and defined scope.

**Q: Will you be managing employees?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I may coordinate with the client's team during implementation, but I'm not filling a management position. My role is to analyze, recommend, and help implement improvements — not to manage the client's day-to-day operations."

**Q: Do you plan to stay permanently?**
> **Ideal answer:** "No. My consulting engagement has a defined end date. I maintain my [home/ties] in Canada and will return upon completion of this project."

### 4. Accountant

**Key context:** Accountants must hold a baccalaureate or licenciatura degree, OR a CPA/CA/CGA professional designation. Officers will verify your credentials match and that your duties are genuinely accounting work (not bookkeeping or general finance).

**Q: Are you a CPA or Chartered Accountant?**
> **Ideal answer:** If yes: "Yes, I hold a [CPA/CA/CGA] designation from [province/state], license number [X]. Here is my credential documentation." If no: "I hold a bachelor's degree in Accounting from [university], which satisfies the USMCA educational requirement for this profession."

**Q: What type of accounting work will you do?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I will perform [financial statement preparation/auditing/tax planning/management accounting/forensic accounting] for [employer]. My duties include [specific accounting activities]." Emphasize professional-level accounting, not bookkeeping or data entry.

**Q: How is your work different from bookkeeping?**
> **Ideal answer:** "Bookkeeping involves recording transactions. As an accountant, I analyze financial data, prepare financial statements, ensure regulatory compliance, provide strategic financial advice, and conduct audits. My work requires professional judgment and expertise."

**Q: Will you need a U.S. CPA license to do this work?**
> **Ideal answer:** "The TN visa requires a baccalaureate degree or professional credential — I meet that through my [degree/Canadian CPA]. Whether I need a U.S. CPA for state-specific work is a separate regulatory matter that my employer has addressed."

**Q: Who will you be reporting to?**
> **Ideal answer:** Name the supervisor/department. Show you understand the organizational structure and that your role is clearly defined.

**Q: Is this a permanent position?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I'm coming to work temporarily for up to [duration on your letter]. I maintain my home and professional ties in Canada." Even though TN can be renewed indefinitely, always frame your intent as temporary.

### 5. Economist

**Key context:** The June 2025 USCIS Policy Manual update narrowed the Economist category. Officers now scrutinize whether your work is genuinely *economic analysis* (qualifying) versus financial analysis, market research, or data analytics (not qualifying). You must hold a baccalaureate degree.

**Q: What does an economist do differently from a financial analyst?**
> **Ideal answer:** "An economist studies economic trends, develops forecasts using economic models, analyzes the impact of government policies, and conducts research on economic phenomena like inflation, employment, and trade. A financial analyst evaluates individual investments or company financials. My work is rooted in economic theory and macroeconomic/microeconomic analysis."

**Q: What is your degree in?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I hold a bachelor's degree in Economics from [university]." A degree specifically in Economics is strongest. Degrees in Finance, Business, or Statistics may face pushback — be prepared to show how your coursework was economics-focused.

**Q: Describe the economic research you'll be conducting.**
> **Ideal answer:** Be specific: "I'll be analyzing [labor market trends/trade policy impacts/pricing models/economic forecasting] for [employer]. My work involves building econometric models, conducting statistical analysis of economic data, and producing research reports that inform [business/policy decisions]."

**Q: Will you be doing market research or data analytics?**
> **Ideal answer:** "My work is economic analysis, not market research. I apply economic theory and econometric methods to study [specific economic questions]. While I use data, my analysis is grounded in economic principles — supply and demand, market equilibrium, policy impact assessment — not consumer behavior surveys or marketing analytics."

**Q: Are you publishing research or working for a private company?**
> **Ideal answer:** Either is fine. "I'll be conducting economic analysis for [employer] to inform their [business strategy/policy recommendations/regulatory compliance]. Economists work in both academic and private sector settings."

**Q: What economic models or tools do you use?**
> **Ideal answer:** Name specific tools: "I use econometric software like Stata, R, or EViews to build regression models, time-series analyses, and forecasting models. My methodology is grounded in [specific economic frameworks relevant to your work]."

### 6. Graphic Designer

**Key context:** Graphic Designers must hold a baccalaureate or licenciatura degree, OR a post-secondary diploma and three years of experience. Officers will verify your work is *design* (qualifying) and not web development, coding, or UX engineering (not qualifying under this category).

**Q: What kind of design work will you do?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I create visual communications — [brand identity, marketing materials, publication layouts, packaging design, advertising campaigns, digital graphics]. My work involves conceptualizing visual solutions, creating original designs, and producing final artwork for [print/digital/both]."

**Q: Do you do any web development or coding?**
> **Ideal answer:** "My role is visual design, not development. I create the visual concepts, layouts, and graphics. I use design tools like Adobe Creative Suite, Figma, or Sketch — not programming languages. If designs are implemented on a website, a developer handles the coding."

**Q: Can I see your portfolio?**
> **Ideal answer:** Have a portfolio ready — physical prints or a tablet with your work loaded (don't rely on internet access). "Here are examples of my design work for [previous clients/employers]." Show professional-quality graphic design, not UI wireframes or code.

**Q: What is your educational background?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I hold a [bachelor's degree in Graphic Design/Visual Communications/Fine Arts] from [university]." Or: "I have a [diploma in Graphic Design] from [college] plus [X] years of professional design experience, which meets the USMCA requirement."

**Q: How is graphic design different from UX design?**
> **Ideal answer:** "Graphic design focuses on visual communication — creating the actual visual elements like logos, layouts, illustrations, and typography. UX design focuses on user experience and interaction flows, which is more of a research and systems role. My work is creating visual content."

**Q: What tools do you use?**
> **Ideal answer:** "Adobe Photoshop, Illustrator, InDesign, Figma for visual design. These are industry-standard graphic design tools." Avoid emphasizing development tools like VS Code or programming frameworks.

### 7. Pharmacist

**Key context:** Pharmacists must hold a baccalaureate or licenciatura degree AND a state/provincial license or degree. U.S. state licensing requirements vary and officers may ask about your ability to practice in the specific state.

**Q: Are you licensed to practice pharmacy?**
> **Ideal answer:** "Yes, I hold a pharmacy license from [province/state]. Here is my license documentation." If you're in the process of obtaining a U.S. state license: "I hold a [Canadian pharmacy degree and license]. I am in the process of obtaining [state] licensure, and my employer is aware of the timeline."

**Q: Which state will you be working in, and do you have that state's license?**
> **Ideal answer:** Be specific about the state. If you have the state license, present it. If not, explain the reciprocity or transfer process and that your employer supports the timeline. Some states require NAPLEX and MPJE exams.

**Q: What pharmacy duties will you perform?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I will [dispense medications, counsel patients on drug interactions, review prescriptions, manage pharmacy inventory, collaborate with physicians on treatment plans]. These are standard pharmacist duties as defined by the profession."

**Q: Do you have FPGEC certification?**
> **Ideal answer:** If applicable: "Yes, I hold FPGEC certification from the NABP, which verifies my foreign pharmacy education meets U.S. standards." This is particularly relevant for pharmacists trained outside the U.S./Canada.

**Q: Will you be supervising pharmacy technicians?**
> **Ideal answer:** "Yes, supervising technicians is a standard part of pharmacist duties. My primary role remains professional pharmacy practice — dispensing, counseling, and clinical review."

### 8. Registered Nurse

**Key context:** Registered Nurses must hold a state/provincial license AND a baccalaureate degree. Additionally, nurses educated outside the U.S. typically need a VisaScreen certificate from CGFNS (Commission on Graduates of Foreign Nursing Schools). This is a common point of denial.

**Q: Do you have a VisaScreen certificate?**
> **Ideal answer:** "Yes, here is my VisaScreen certificate from CGFNS, which verifies my nursing education, license, and English proficiency meet U.S. standards." **This is critical — without it, you will likely be denied.**

**Q: Are you licensed as an RN?**
> **Ideal answer:** "Yes, I hold an RN license from [province]. I have also [obtained/am in the process of obtaining] licensure in [U.S. state where I'll work]." Present your license documentation.

**Q: Do you have a bachelor's degree in nursing?**
> **Ideal answer:** "Yes, I hold a Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BScN/BSN) from [university]." Note: a diploma or associate's degree alone is insufficient for TN — you need the baccalaureate.

**Q: What clinical duties will you perform?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I will provide direct patient care including [assessment, medication administration, care planning, patient education, coordination with physicians]. These are standard registered nursing duties."

**Q: Have you passed the NCLEX-RN?**
> **Ideal answer:** If yes: "Yes, I have passed the NCLEX-RN examination." Many U.S. states require this for licensure, and having it strengthens your application significantly.

**Q: Will you be working in a hospital or clinic?**
> **Ideal answer:** Be specific about the healthcare setting and your role within it. "I'll be working at [facility name] in the [department], providing [type of nursing care]."

### 9. Architect

**Key context:** Architects must hold a baccalaureate or licenciatura degree OR a state/provincial license. Officers verify that your work involves *building design* (qualifying), not interior design, landscape architecture, or construction management (not qualifying under this category).

**Q: Are you a licensed architect?**
> **Ideal answer:** If yes: "Yes, I'm a licensed architect in [province/state], registration number [X]." If no: "I hold a professional Bachelor of Architecture (B.Arch) degree from [university], which is an accredited architecture program and satisfies the USMCA requirement."

**Q: What type of architectural work will you do?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I will design [buildings/structures] — creating architectural plans, developing design concepts, producing construction documents, and ensuring compliance with building codes and zoning regulations. My work involves the design of [residential/commercial/institutional] buildings."

**Q: Will you be doing interior design or landscape work?**
> **Ideal answer:** "No, my work is building architecture — the design of structures and buildings. Interior design and landscape architecture are separate disciplines. My duties focus on building design, structural planning, and construction documentation."

**Q: Do you need a U.S. architecture license to practice?**
> **Ideal answer:** "The TN visa requires a degree or license to qualify. I meet that through my [degree/Canadian license]. U.S. state licensing for practice is a separate regulatory matter — my employer has structured my role appropriately."

**Q: What software do you use?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I use industry-standard architecture tools — AutoCAD, Revit, SketchUp, ArchiCAD, and BIM software for building design and construction documentation."

**Q: Will you be stamping drawings?**
> **Ideal answer:** If licensed in the U.S. state: "Yes, I'm licensed to stamp drawings in [state]." If not: "My drawings will be reviewed and stamped by a U.S.-licensed architect at the firm. My role is design and documentation under their supervision."

### 10. Scientific Technician/Technologist

**Key context:** This is the other heavily scrutinized "catch-all" category alongside Management Consultant. The June 2025 USCIS update reinforced strict requirements: (1) you must have theoretical knowledge in one of 10 specified disciplines (agricultural sciences, astronomy, biology, chemistry, engineering, forestry, geology, geophysics, meteorology, physics), (2) you must work under the *direct supervision* of a degreed professional in that discipline, and (3) you must exercise *independent judgment* — not just follow manuals. Patient care is explicitly excluded.

**Q: Who is your supervising professional?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I will work under the direct supervision of [name], who holds a [bachelor's/master's/PhD] degree in [one of the 10 qualifying disciplines] and serves as [title] at [company]. They will manage, coordinate, and review my work." **Have the supervisor's credentials documented.**

**Q: What is your educational background in science?**
> **Ideal answer:** "I hold a [degree/diploma] in [relevant discipline] from [institution]." If qualifying through experience: "I have [X] years of progressive experience in [discipline], which has given me theoretical knowledge equivalent to formal education. I can provide detailed documentation of my training and experience."

**Q: Will you be exercising independent judgment?**
> **Ideal answer:** "Yes. While I work under the direction of [supervisor], I apply theoretical knowledge to solve practical problems — [give specific examples: formulating solutions, conducting quality control analysis, adapting procedures to field conditions, troubleshooting equipment]. I don't simply follow step-by-step manuals."

**Q: Will you be providing patient care?**
> **Ideal answer:** "No. My work is in [scientific/technical field], not healthcare. I will not be providing any patient care." **This is critical — patient care disqualifies you from this category regardless of your scientific background.**

**Q: What scientific discipline does your work fall under?**
> **Ideal answer:** Name one of the 10 qualifying disciplines specifically. "My work falls under [chemistry/biology/engineering/etc.]. I apply principles of [discipline] to [specific technical work]."

**Q: How is your work different from manual labor or assembly?**
> **Ideal answer:** "My work requires applying theoretical scientific knowledge to solve problems. For example, [describe a situation where you analyzed data, made a judgment call, or adapted a scientific approach]. This is fundamentally different from following assembly instructions or performing repetitive tasks."

**Q: Can your supervisor verify your role?**
> **Ideal answer:** "Yes. My supervisor [name] is available at [phone/email] and can confirm my duties, the supervision structure, and how my work supports their professional practice." Have this contact information ready.

## Body Language & Behaviour Tips

Your appearance and demeanour matter more than you think. CBP officers make rapid assessments, and first impressions influence how thoroughly they scrutinize your application. You are applying for a *professional* work visa — look and act like a professional.

### How to Present Yourself

- **Project calm confidence.** You have every right to apply for TN status. You're a qualified professional with proper documentation. Approach the interaction as a routine professional matter, not an interrogation.
- **Be friendly but not overly casual.** A warm "Good morning" and a smile set the right tone. Don't crack jokes, ramble, or try to be the officer's friend.
- **Stand or sit up straight.** Good posture signals confidence and professionalism. Avoid slouching, leaning on the counter, or fidgeting.

### Eye Contact, Posture, Tone

- **Maintain natural eye contact** when speaking and listening. Looking away constantly can signal dishonesty; staring intensely can seem aggressive. Aim for the natural rhythm of a professional conversation.
- **Speak clearly and at a moderate pace.** Don't rush your answers. Don't mumble. If English isn't your first language, slow down slightly — clarity matters more than speed.
- **Keep your tone even and respectful.** Never raise your voice, argue, or show frustration — even if the officer seems skeptical or asks the same question multiple times.
- **Listen fully before answering.** Never interrupt the officer. Wait until they finish their question, pause briefly, then respond.

### What to Wear

- **Business casual is the standard.** For men: dress pants or chinos, a collared shirt (button-down or polo), clean shoes. For women: dress pants or a skirt with a professional top, or a business-casual dress.
- **Avoid:** ripped jeans, tank tops, flip-flops, athletic wear, graphic t-shirts, hats, sunglasses indoors.
- **You don't need a suit**, but you should look like someone who works in a professional office. The TN visa is for professionals — dress the part.
- **Grooming matters.** Clean, neat appearance. Well-groomed hair. No strong cologne or perfume.

### How to Handle Nervousness

- **Prepare thoroughly.** The #1 cure for nervousness is knowing your material cold. Review your job duties, your qualifications, and your employer letter until you can discuss them naturally.
- **Practice your answers out loud** with a friend or family member. Rehearse the most likely questions until your responses feel natural, not scripted.
- **Breathe.** If you feel anxious at the counter, take a slow breath before answering. A brief pause looks thoughtful, not nervous.
- **It's okay to be a little nervous.** Officers expect it. What they don't want to see is evasiveness, inconsistency, or hostility.
- **Bring water.** Especially at land borders where you may wait for hours. Dehydration makes anxiety worse.

### Cultural Considerations

- **Be direct and concise.** North American communication style values clear, straightforward answers. Don't give long, winding explanations when a short answer will do.
- **Answer only what's asked.** Volunteering extra information can open new lines of questioning. If the officer wants more detail, they'll ask.
- **"I don't know" is acceptable** for questions outside your knowledge — but never for questions about your own job, qualifications, or employer.
- **Respect the authority dynamic.** The officer has discretion over your entry. Be polite and cooperative, even if you disagree with a line of questioning.
- **If you don't understand a question**, say: "I'm sorry, could you please repeat that?" or "Could you clarify what you mean?" This is far better than guessing.

## Document Organisation Checklist

A well-organized document package signals professionalism and preparation. Officers process hundreds of applications — make yours easy to review.

### Cover Sheet Template

Place this on top of your document package:

```
TN VISA APPLICATION — COVER SHEET

Applicant Name:        ____________________________
Citizenship:           ☐ Canadian  ☐ Mexican
TN Profession:         ____________________________
U.S. Employer:         ____________________________
Employer Address:      ____________________________
Start Date:            ____________________________
Duration:              ____________________________
Salary:                ____________________________

Documents Enclosed:
☐ Employer support letter
☐ Passport (valid 6+ months)
☐ Degree/diploma (original + copy)
☐ Transcripts
☐ Professional license/certification
☐ Resume/CV
☐ Previous TN approval (I-94), if applicable
☐ Credential evaluation (if non-U.S./Canadian degree)
☐ Additional supporting documents: ________________
```

### How to Organise Your Package

Use a **clean, professional folder or binder** with tabbed sections:

1. **Tab 1 — Cover Sheet** (see above)
2. **Tab 2 — Employer Support Letter** (the most important document)
3. **Tab 3 — Educational Credentials** (degree, transcripts, credential evaluation)
4. **Tab 4 — Professional Licenses/Certifications**
5. **Tab 5 — Resume/CV**
6. **Tab 6 — Previous TN Approvals** (I-94 records, prior approval notices)
7. **Tab 7 — Supporting Documents** (job description, consulting agreement, portfolio, reference letters)
8. **Tab 8 — Dependent Documents** (TD visa materials for spouse/children, if applicable)

### What Order to Present Documents

When the officer asks for your documents:

1. **Hand them the employer support letter first** — this is the document they'll read most carefully.
2. **Then your passport** (open to the photo page).
3. **Then your degree/credentials** — originals, not copies.
4. **Let them ask for anything else.** Don't dump the entire folder on the counter. Present documents as requested.

### How Many Copies to Bring

| Document | Originals | Copies |
|----------|-----------|--------|
| Employer support letter | 1 | 3 |
| Degree/diploma | 1 (original) | 2 |
| Transcripts | 1 (official) | 2 |
| Professional license | 1 (original) | 2 |
| Resume/CV | — | 3 |
| Credential evaluation | 1 (original) | 2 |
| Previous I-94 | 1 (printout) | 2 |

**Why multiple copies?** The officer may keep one for their file. If you're sent to secondary inspection, a different officer may review your case. Having extras prevents delays.

### What to Keep in Your Carry-On vs. Checked Luggage

**CARRY-ON (always accessible):**
- ✅ Entire document package
- ✅ Passport
- ✅ Phone with employer contact information
- ✅ Printed copies of all documents
- ✅ Pen (for filling out forms)
- ✅ Water and snacks (for long waits)

**CHECKED LUGGAGE:**
- ✅ Personal belongings, clothing
- ⚠️ Do NOT check any immigration documents
- ⚠️ Do NOT pack items that suggest permanent relocation (see Emergency Scenarios below)

**Critical rule:** Never put any immigration document in checked luggage. If your bag is lost or delayed, you cannot apply without your documents.

## Airport vs. Land Border Comparison

Canadians can apply for TN status at any U.S. port of entry — airports with pre-clearance or land border crossings. Each has trade-offs.

### Side-by-Side Comparison

| Factor | Airport (Pre-Clearance) | Land Border |
|--------|------------------------|-------------|
| **Wait time** | 30 min – 2 hours (plus flight schedule) | 1 – 4+ hours (variable, especially during shutdown) |
| **Cost** | Flight ticket + $6 I-94 fee | Gas/transit + $6 I-94 fee |
| **If denied** | You don't board the plane; stay in Canada | You turn around and drive home |
| **Officer experience with TN** | Moderate — varies by airport | High at major crossings (Buffalo, Detroit, Blaine) |
| **Scheduling** | Tied to your flight time | Flexible (some ports now require online scheduling) |
| **Secondary inspection** | Available but may delay your flight | No flight to miss; more time for review |
| **Stress level** | Higher — flight pressure, public setting | Lower — you can try again same day if needed |
| **Document review depth** | Often quicker due to flight schedules | Can be more thorough |

### Which Is Safer for First-Time Applicants?

**Land border is generally recommended for first-time TN applicants**, for several reasons:

- **No flight at risk.** If you're denied or sent to secondary, you haven't lost a plane ticket. You drive home and regroup.
- **You can try again.** At a land border, if denied, you can return the same day or next day with improved documentation (though this carries risk — see Emergency Scenarios).
- **More time for review.** Officers at busy land ports like Buffalo Peace Bridge process many TN applications and are familiar with the categories.
- **Less pressure.** No boarding time, no gate announcements, no line of passengers behind you.

**Airport pre-clearance is better when:**
- You've been approved before and are renewing
- Your case is straightforward (clear degree match, common profession)
- You're already flying for other reasons
- You're using Billy Bishop Toronto (new pre-clearance as of March 2026) for convenience

### Which Is Faster?

- **Airport:** Faster if your case is simple and you're already flying. Pre-clearance adds 30–60 minutes to your airport arrival time.
- **Land border:** Faster for the TN process itself (no flight overhead), but wait times vary wildly. Peak hours (7–9 AM, 4–6 PM) can mean 2+ hour waits before you even reach an officer. Off-peak (mid-morning, mid-week) is fastest.

### Cost Comparison

| Route | Typical Cost |
|-------|-------------|
| **Land border (driving)** | Gas ($20–60) + I-94 fee ($6) = **$26–66** |
| **Airport (flying)** | Flight ($150–500+) + I-94 fee ($6) = **$156–506+** |
| **Airport (if denied)** | Flight cost is lost; may need to rebook = **$300–1,000+** |
| **Land border (if denied)** | Gas only; can retry = **$26–66** |

**Bottom line:** Land border is cheaper and lower-risk for first-timers. Airport is more convenient for renewals and straightforward cases.

## Emergency Scenarios

These are the situations that cause the most panic. Having a plan for each one dramatically reduces your risk.

### What if the officer seems skeptical?

**Stay calm and professional.** Skepticism is part of the job — it doesn't mean you'll be denied.

- **Don't get defensive.** Answer their questions directly and provide additional documentation if you have it.
- **Refer to your employer letter.** "My employer letter on page [X] describes my specific duties in detail. Would you like me to walk you through them?"
- **Offer to clarify.** "I'd be happy to explain how my qualifications match the [profession] category."
- **Don't argue or debate immigration law.** Even if you think the officer is wrong, arguing will not help. Stay cooperative.

### What if they ask about green card plans?

**This is one of the most dangerous questions.** TN status does not allow "dual intent" — unlike H-1B, you cannot simultaneously hold TN status and pursue a green card.

- **If you have no green card plans:** "I have no plans to apply for permanent residence. I'm coming to work temporarily and will return to Canada when my assignment ends."
- **If your employer has mentioned sponsorship (but nothing filed):** "I'm focused on this temporary assignment. I have no pending immigration applications."
- **Never say:** "My employer is going to sponsor me for a green card" or "I hope to stay permanently." This will result in denial.
- **If you have a pending I-140:** This is extremely risky for TN. Consult an immigration attorney before attempting a border crossing. Many attorneys recommend switching to H-1B (which allows dual intent) before filing green card paperwork.

### What if they say your degree doesn't match?

- **Stay calm.** Ask: "Could you tell me specifically what concern you have about my degree? I may have additional documentation that addresses it."
- **Present supporting evidence:** credential evaluation, course transcripts showing relevant coursework, professional certifications, or letters from your university.
- **If your degree title is ambiguous** (e.g., "Bachelor of Science" without specifying the major), your transcript showing your major and coursework is critical.
- **If the officer is firm:** Consider requesting withdrawal (see below) rather than accepting a denial.

### What if they want to deny you?

**Before a formal denial is issued, you have a critical window to request withdrawal of your application.**

A denial goes on your immigration record and can complicate future applications. A withdrawal is far less damaging.

- **Watch for signals:** If the officer says things like "I'm not going to be able to approve this" or "I don't think this qualifies," this is your moment.
- **Politely say:** "Officer, if you're not inclined to approve my application, I would like to request permission to withdraw my application for admission."
- **Be respectful.** Withdrawal is *discretionary* — the officer can grant or deny your request. Being polite and cooperative increases your chances.

### How to Request Withdrawal Instead of Denial

Withdrawal of application for admission is governed by DHS discretionary authority. Here's what you need to know:

1. **You must ask before a formal denial is issued.** Once the officer stamps "denied," it's too late.
2. **Say clearly:** "I would like to withdraw my application for admission and return to Canada."
3. **The officer will have you complete Form I-275** (Withdrawal of Application for Admission/Consular Notification).
4. **You will be required to depart immediately** — typically you'll be escorted back to the Canadian side.
5. **Withdrawal goes on your record** but is far less serious than a denial. It does not trigger reentry bars.
6. **You can reapply** after addressing the issues — with better documentation, a revised employer letter, or legal counsel.

**Key difference:**
- **Denial** = formal finding of ineligibility → on your record → complicates future applications → may trigger additional scrutiny
- **Withdrawal** = voluntary departure → on your record but less damaging → no formal finding against you → easier to reapply

### What if you're sent to secondary inspection?

**Secondary inspection is not a denial.** It's a more thorough review of your application. Many successful TN applicants go through secondary.

- **Stay calm.** Being sent to secondary often just means the primary officer wants a specialist to review your case, or the case is more complex than usual.
- **Bring everything.** This is where your extra copies and supporting documents pay off. The secondary officer may ask for documents the primary officer didn't.
- **Expect a longer wait.** Secondary can take 30 minutes to several hours. During the 2026 DHS shutdown, waits have been even longer.
- **You may be interviewed again.** Answer consistently with what you told the primary officer. Inconsistencies between primary and secondary interviews are a major red flag.
- **Your phone and belongings may be inspected** (see below).

### What if they search your phone?

**CBP has broad authority to search electronic devices at the border.** This is legal and increasingly common under enhanced vetting policies.

- **Before you travel:**
  - Review your text messages, emails, and social media for anything that contradicts your TN application (e.g., messages about "moving permanently," green card discussions, job searching beyond your TN role).
  - Remove or archive sensitive personal content you'd rather not share.
  - Log out of social media apps if you prefer (though officers may ask you to log in).
  - **Do not delete messages about your TN application** — this looks suspicious if they can tell content was recently deleted.

- **During the search:**
  - Cooperate. Refusing a device search can result in denial of entry and seizure of the device.
  - If asked about specific messages or content, answer honestly.
  - Officers are looking for evidence of: immigrant intent, unauthorized work, misrepresentation, or security concerns.

- **Best practice:** Keep your phone "border clean" — nothing that contradicts your temporary intent or professional qualifications.

### What if you're travelling with moving boxes?

**This is a classic red flag for immigrant intent.** Showing up at the border with a moving truck full of household goods signals you're relocating permanently, not coming temporarily.

- **Ideal approach:** Ship your belongings separately *after* you've been admitted. Use a moving company or mail items to your U.S. address.
- **If you must bring items:** Keep it minimal. A suitcase or two is normal. Multiple boxes of household goods, furniture, or a fully loaded vehicle is not.
- **If asked:** "I'm bringing personal items for my temporary stay. My permanent home remains in Canada at [address]." Be prepared to show ties to Canada (lease, property ownership, family).
- **What to avoid bringing through the border:**
  - ❌ Furniture or large household items
  - ❌ Boxes labeled "kitchen," "bedroom," etc.
  - ❌ Family photo albums and sentimental items (suggests permanent move)
  - ❌ Pets (complicated and signals permanence)
  - ❌ Your entire wardrobe
- **What's fine:**
  - ✅ 1-2 suitcases of clothing
  - ✅ A laptop and work equipment
  - ✅ Professional documents and books
  - ✅ Toiletries and personal essentials

## 2026 Enhanced Vetting Preparation

The immigration landscape has shifted significantly since late 2025. Multiple new policies affect TN applicants directly. Here's what you need to know and how to prepare.

### USCIS Vetting Centre (December 2025)

In December 2025, USCIS announced a new centralized **Vetting Center in Atlanta** dedicated to "enhanced screening" of immigrants and visa applicants. The center focuses on:

- **National security screening** — cross-referencing applicants against expanded databases
- **Fraud detection** — verifying employer letters, job offers, and credentials more thoroughly
- **Social media and online presence review** — checking public profiles for inconsistencies
- **"Anti-American" concerns** — a vaguely defined category that has raised concerns among immigration attorneys

**What this means for TN applicants:**
- USCIS-filed petitions (I-129 extensions, changes of status) may face longer processing times as cases are routed through the Vetting Center.
- Border applications (CBP) are not directly processed by the Vetting Center, but CBP officers have access to enhanced screening tools and may apply similar scrutiny.
- Ensure your application is airtight — any inconsistency between your documents, online presence, and interview answers will be flagged.

### Social Media Checks — What to Clean Up

The State Department and USCIS now require disclosure of **five years of social media handles** for many visa categories. While TN border applications don't have a formal social media disclosure form, CBP officers can and do check social media during inspections.

**Before your border crossing, review and clean up:**

- **LinkedIn:** Ensure your profile matches your TN application exactly — same job title, same employer, same duties. Remove any references to "permanent" roles, green card sponsorship, or job searching for non-TN positions.
- **Facebook/Instagram:** Remove or make private any posts about "moving to America permanently," "starting a new life in the U.S.," or similar language that contradicts temporary intent.
- **Twitter/X:** Review posts about immigration, work, or relocation. Delete anything that could be interpreted as immigrant intent.
- **Job boards:** If you have active profiles on Indeed, LinkedIn Jobs, or Glassdoor, ensure they don't show you searching for jobs outside your TN role.
- **Reddit/forums:** If you've posted in immigration forums about green card strategies or permanent relocation, consider deleting those posts.
- **Set profiles to private** if you're concerned about content you can't easily review.

**What officers look for:**
- Contradictions between your stated temporary intent and social media posts suggesting permanence
- Evidence of unauthorized work or job searching
- Posts about green card applications or permanent immigration plans
- Connections to individuals or organizations flagged for security concerns

### DHS Shutdown Impact on Wait Times

The 2026 DHS shutdown — which began January 31, 2026 and lasted 76 days (the longest in U.S. history) — had significant impacts on TN processing:

- **CBP continued processing TN applications** at the border throughout the shutdown (border inspections are "essential" services).
- **Wait times increased dramatically** due to staffing shortages, with TSA reporting the "highest wait times in TSA history."
- **Enhanced vetting added further delays** — more applicants were sent to secondary inspection.
- **USCIS remained open** (fee-funded) for extensions and changes of status filed inside the U.S.
- **The Department of Labor went offline**, blocking H-1B LCA filings — this made TN even more attractive for eligible professionals, increasing border volume.

**As of April 30, 2026:** The DHS shutdown has just ended with President Trump signing a bipartisan funding bill. However, the backlog effects will linger for weeks. Expect:
- Continued longer-than-normal wait times at ports of entry
- Possible staffing gaps as furloughed workers return
- Enhanced vetting procedures that were implemented during the shutdown are likely to remain permanent

**Planning advice:**
- Build in 2–3 extra hours for any border crossing
- Avoid peak hours (7–9 AM, 4–6 PM)
- Avoid Fridays and long weekends
- Mid-week, mid-morning crossings have the shortest waits

### Billy Bishop Toronto Pre-Clearance (New — March 2026)

As of **March 10, 2026**, Billy Bishop Toronto City Airport (YTZ) now has a **U.S. Customs and Border Protection pre-clearance facility**. This is the ninth Canadian airport — and the first since 2016 — to offer pre-clearance.

**What this means for TN applicants:**
- You can now apply for TN status at Billy Bishop before boarding a flight to the U.S.
- This is a major convenience for Toronto-area professionals who previously had to drive to a land border or use Pearson (YYZ).
- Pre-clearance means you clear U.S. immigration *before* departure — you arrive in the U.S. as a domestic passenger.

**Considerations:**
- Billy Bishop is a smaller airport with limited U.S. destinations (primarily Porter Airlines to Newark, Boston, Chicago, Washington).
- Pre-clearance officers at new facilities may be less experienced with TN applications than officers at established ports like Buffalo or Pearson.
- For first-time TN applications, the land border may still be safer (no flight at risk if denied).
- For renewals and straightforward cases, Billy Bishop is an excellent new option.

### CBP Buffalo Online Scheduling Requirement

As of **June 2025**, CBP at the Port of Buffalo (Peace Bridge) now requires **all USMCA-related appointments — including TN and L visa applications — to be scheduled entirely online**.

**What you need to know:**
- You can no longer walk up to Buffalo without an appointment for TN processing.
- Schedule your appointment at [cbp.gov/contact/ports/buffalo](https://www.cbp.gov/about/contact/ports/buffalo-new-york-0901) using their online scheduling system.
- **Book at least two weeks in advance** to secure a slot. Popular times fill up quickly.
- Changes and rescheduling are also done online — no phone calls.
- This applies to initial TN applications, employer changes, and category changes at Buffalo.

**Other land border ports** (e.g., Detroit/Ambassador Bridge, Blaine/Pacific Highway, Champlain) may have different scheduling requirements. Check the specific port's CBP page before travelling.

**Pro tip:** If Buffalo appointments are fully booked, consider:
- Rainbow Bridge (Niagara Falls) — different port, may have availability
- Lewiston-Queenston Bridge — another Niagara option
- Other ports that don't yet require online scheduling
